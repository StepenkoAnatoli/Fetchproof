 The **percent sign** % (sometimes **per cent sign** in [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English)) is the symbol used to indicate a [percentage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percentage), a number or [ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio) as a [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_(mathematics)) of 100. Related signs include the [permille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permille) (per thousand) sign ‰ and the [permyriad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_point#Permyriad) (per ten thousand) sign ‱ . Higher proportions use [parts-per notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts-per_notation).





## Correct style

 [ [edit](/w/index.php?title=Percent_sign&action=edit&section=1) ]



### Form and spacing

 [ [edit](/w/index.php?title=Percent_sign&action=edit&section=2) ]

 English style guides prescribe writing the percent sign following the number without any space between (e.g. 50%). [[1]](#cite_note-1) However, the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) and [ISO 31-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_31-0) standard prescribe a space between the number and percent sign, [[2]](#cite_note-2) [[3]](#cite_note-3) [[4]](#cite_note-4) in line with the general practice of using a [non-breaking space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-breaking_space) between a [numerical value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_value) and its corresponding [unit of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement).

 Other languages have other rules for spacing in front of the percent sign:


- In [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language), the percent sign is placed before the number and is spaced. [[5]](#cite_note-5)

- In [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_language) and in [Slovak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_language), the percent sign is spaced with a non-breaking space if the number is used as a noun. [[6]](#cite_note-6) In Czech, no space is inserted if the number is used as an adjective (e.g. "a 50% increase"), [[7]](#cite_note-7) whereas Slovak uses a   non-breaking space in this case as well. [[8]](#cite_note-8)

- In [Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_language), the percent sign is spaced [[9]](#cite_note-9) with a non-breaking space.

- In [Finnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_language), the percent sign is always spaced, and a [case suffix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_noun_cases) can be attached to it using the [colon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_(punctuation)) (e.g. *50   %:n kasvu* ' an increase of 50% ' ). [[10]](#cite_note-10)

- In [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), the percent sign must be spaced with a non-breaking space. [[11]](#cite_note-11) [[12]](#cite_note-12)

- According to the [Real Academia Española](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Academia_Española), in [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language), the percent sign should be spaced. [[13]](#cite_note-13) Despite that, in North American Spanish ([Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish) and the [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_United_States)), several style guides and institutions either recommend the percent sign be written following the number without any space between or do so in their own publications in accordance with common usage in that region. [[14]](#cite_note-14) [[15]](#cite_note-15)

- In [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language), the percent sign is rarely spaced, contrary to the guidelines of the GOST 8.417-2002 state standard.

- According to the [Swedish Language Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Language_Council), the percent sign should be preceded by a space in [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language), as all other units.

- In [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), the space is prescribed by the regulatory body in the national standard [DIN 5008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DIN_5008).

- In [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language) and some other [Turkic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages), the percent sign precedes rather than follows the number, without an intervening space.

- In [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language) texts, the percent sign may either precede or follow the number, in either case without a space.

- In [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language), the percent sign follows the number; as Arabic is written from [right to left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_left), this means that the percent sign is to the left of the number, usually without a space.

- In [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language), the percent sign is written to the right of the number, just as in English, without an intervening space. This is because numbers in Hebrew (which otherwise is written from right to left) are written from left to right, as in English.

- In [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language), the official rule ([NBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Normalization) Z 01-002) is to place a space between the number and the sign (e.g. *een stijging van 50   %* ), but most of the time, the space is missing (e.g. *een stijging van 50%* ). [[16]](#cite_note-16)



## Evolution

 [ [edit](/w/index.php?title=Percent_sign&action=edit&section=3) ]

 Prior to 1425, there is no known evidence of a special symbol being used for percentages. The [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language) term *per cento*, "for a hundred", was used as well as several different abbreviations (e.g. "per 100", "p 100", "p cento"). Examples of this can be seen in the 1339 arithmetic text (author unknown) depicted below. [[17]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1898p._437-17) The letter p with its [descender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descender) crossed by a horizontal or diagonal stroke (to indicate abbreviation) [conventionally stood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribal_abbreviation) for per, por, par, or pur in Medieval and Renaissance [palaeography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeography). [[18]](#cite_note-18)

 1339 arithmetic text in *Rara Arithmetica*, p. 437



 At some point, a scribe used the abbreviation *pc* with a tiny loop or circle (depicting the ending *-o* used in [Italian ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masculine_ordinal), as in *primo* , *secondo* ; it is analogous to the English *-th* as in *25th*). This appears in some additional pages of a 1425 text which were probably added around 1435. [[19]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1898pp._439–440-19)

 1425 arithmetic text in *Rara Arithmetica*, p. 440 [[20]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1898p._440-20)



 The *pc* with a loop eventually evolved into a horizontal fraction sign by 1650 (see below for an example in a 1684 text) [[21]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1898p._441-21) and thereafter lost the *per*. [[20]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1898p._440-20)

 1684 arithmetic text in *Rara Arithmetica*, p. 441



 In 1925, D. E. Smith wrote, "The [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus_(punctuation)) form ( ) is modern." [[22]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESmith1925Vol._2,_p._250-22)

## Other uses

 [ [edit](/w/index.php?title=Percent_sign&action=edit&section=7) ]



### In computers

 [ [edit](/w/index.php?title=Percent_sign&action=edit&section=8) ]

 Names for the percent sign include percent sign (in [ITU-T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITU-T)), [mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_operation), grapes (in [hacker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacker_(programmer_subculture)) jargon), [[24]](#cite_note-24) and the humorous double-oh-seven (in [INTERCAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INTERCAL)).

 In [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing), the percent [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_(computing)) is also used for the [modulo operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_operation) in [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_language) that derive their syntax from the [C programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_(programming_language)), which in turn acquired this usage from the earlier [B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_(programming_language)). [[25]](#cite_note-25)

 In the textual representation of [URIs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Resource_Identifier), a `%` immediately followed by a 2-digit [hexadecimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexadecimal) number denotes an octet specifying (part of) a character that might otherwise not be allowed in URIs (see [percent-encoding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percent-encoding)).

 In [SQL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL), the percent sign is a [wildcard character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildcard_character) in "LIKE" expressions, for example ` SELECT * FROM table WHERE fullname LIKE 'Lisa   %' ` will fetch all records whose names start with " Lisa ".

 In [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX) (and therefore also in [LaTeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaTeX)) and [PostScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PostScript), and in [GNU Octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Octave) and [MATLAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MATLAB), [[26]](#cite_note-26) a ` % ` denotes a line [comment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comment_(computing)).

 In [BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASIC), [Visual Basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Basic), [ASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Server_Pages), and [VBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Basic_for_Applications) a trailing `%` after a variable name marks it as an [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer).

 In [ASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Server_Pages), the percent sign can be used to indicate the start and end of the ASP code `"},"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"code","href":"./Template:Code"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"<%...... %>"}},"i":0}}]}'><%......   %>`

 In [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl) `%` is the [sigil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigil_(computer_programming)) for [hashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_table).

 In many programming languages' string formatting operations (performed by functions such as `[printf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printf)` and `[scanf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanf)`), the percent sign denotes parts of the template string that will be replaced with arguments. (See [printf format string](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printf_format_string).) In [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_(programming_language)) and [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_(programming_language)) the percent sign is also used as the string formatting operator. [[27]](#cite_note-27) [[28]](#cite_note-28) [[29]](#cite_note-29)

 In the [command processors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_processor) [COMMAND.COM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMMAND.COM) (DOS) and [CMD.EXE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMD.EXE) ([OS/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OS/2) and Windows), ` %1 `, ` %2 `,   ... stand for the first, second, etc. parameters of a [batch file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batch_file). ` %0 ` stands for the specification of the batch file itself as typed on the command line. The   % sign is also used similarly in the ` FOR ` command.
` %VAR1% ` represents the value of an [environment variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_variable) named `VAR1`. Thus:
` set PATH = c:\; %PATH% `
sets a new value for `PATH`, that being the old value preceded by "`c:\;`".
Because these uses give the percent sign special meaning, the sequence ` %% ` (two percent signs) is used to represent a literal percent sign, so that:
` set PATH = c:\; %% PATH %% `
would set `PATH` to the literal value "`c:\;%PATH%`".

 In the [C Shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_Shell) and [Z shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_shell), `%` is part of the default [command prompt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prompt_(computing)).



### In linguistics

 [ [edit](/w/index.php?title=Percent_sign&action=edit&section=9) ]

 In linguistics, the percent sign is prepended to an example sentence or other [string](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_(computer_science)) to show that it is judged [well-formed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-formedness) (grammatical) by some speakers and ill-formed by others. This may be due to differences in [dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialect) or even individual [idiolects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiolect). [[30]](#cite_note-30) [[31]](#cite_note-31) This use is similar to those of the [asterisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asterisk#Ungrammaticality) to mark ill-formed strings, the [question mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_mark#Linguistics) to mark strings where well-formedness is unclear, and the [number sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_sign#Other_uses) to mark strings that are syntactically well-formed but semantically or pragmatically nonsensical.
